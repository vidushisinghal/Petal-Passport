## 1. Roses — Ooty Rose Garden, Tamil Nadu
*(approx 163 words · ~65 sec)*

Few gardens in India hold this kind of scale. Ooty's rose garden spreads across terraced hillsides — over twenty thousand plants, more than two thousand varieties, all perched at seven thousand feet above sea level. The mornings here are cool in a way only these hills can manage, and the roses hold onto the dew like small held breaths. Go before nine, when the fragrance is strongest and the gardeners haven't yet started their rounds. You smell them before you see them. There are Damasks the color of old photographs, climbers twisted around wooden arches, deep red hybrid teas that look almost black at sunrise. Wear a cardigan and walk slowly, bending now and then to read the little metal name tags. The garden is easy to reach: a morning train from Coimbatore, a flask of hot chai at the gate, and the whole afternoon to wander. No hurry, no plan, just the long rows and the smell of everything opening at once.

## 2. Tulips — Skagit Valley, Washington
*(approx 222 words · ~89 sec)*

Skagit Valley is an hour north of Seattle, a flat patchwork of farmland wedged between the Cascade mountains and the Salish Sea. Every April it turns into ribbons of color — whole fields of red, yellow, purple, orange, row after perfect row, stretching for miles. The farmers here have been growing bulbs for four generations, descended from Dutch immigrants who arrived in the early 1900s. The valley now ships tulip bulbs all over the world.

Go on a weekday morning if you can — the weekends fill quickly. Beaver Marsh Road lets you walk the field edges without the crowds. Tulip Town and RoozenGaarde are the two main farms with proper visitor experiences, gift shops, and demonstration gardens. The Skagit Valley Tulip Festival runs the entire month of April, with bloom timing announced fresh each year on the festival's website — the Pacific Northwest weather can shift the peak by a week or two.

Bring a thermos of something warm. April mornings here can still surprise you with cold mist, and then the sun breaks through and the whole valley lights up. Stop at one of the small farm stands for cardamom tea and a slice of apple pie. The Cascade peaks watch over the fields in the distance, and the tulips in the foreground feel almost too vivid to be real.

## 3. Cherry Blossoms — Tidal Basin, Washington DC
*(approx 179 words · ~72 sec)*

Washington's cherry trees were a gift from Tokyo to the United States in 1912, three thousand Somei Yoshino carried across the Pacific by ship. They still bloom every spring around the Tidal Basin, under the same sky where they were first planted. At peak, the whole basin becomes a pale pink horizon — you walk the two-mile loop and the blossoms meet over your head the whole way. The best thing to do is rent a paddle boat and go out on the water at golden hour. The Jefferson Memorial sits white and solemn across the basin, and the cherry trees lean out over the water, and everything reflects — stone, petals, slow current, the small birds and the people on the paths. Come early — seven in the morning if you can — or late, when the light softens and the crowds thin. The trees only hold their flowers for about two weeks, and you'll want every hour of it. Bring a thermos of something warm. Washington in early spring can still surprise you with a cold morning.

## 4. California Poppies — Antelope Valley, California
*(approx 180 words · ~72 sec)*

In a good year the Antelope Valley turns into a sea of orange. California poppies — the state flower — cover whole hillsides by the millions, and from the top of the seven-mile Antelope Loop Trail you can see the poppies rippling in the wind like a living coat. The reserve is about an hour and a half north of Los Angeles, in the western Mojave Desert, and the weather there is cool in March and April, often windy, sometimes misty at dawn. The south-facing slopes bloom first. Walk slowly — poppies close up at midday heat and in the wind, so catch them in the early morning or late afternoon when the petals are fully open and the low sun is lighting them from within. Pack water and a hat. The reserve has no food, so bring a sandwich and eat it on one of the benches looking out over Antelope Buttes. Some years the bloom is modest; other years it's the kind of landscape you think only happens in paintings. Check the daily bloom report before you go.

## 5. Ranunculus — The Flower Fields, Carlsbad
*(approx 189 words · ~76 sec)*

Fifty acres of ranunculus sit on a hill above the Pacific in Carlsbad, California — a ribboned patchwork of red, yellow, pink, orange, white, and apricot that's been coming back every March for nearly a hundred years. The Tecolote ranunculus was developed here by a family that bred it from wild Asian varieties, and you won't see this scale of the flower anywhere else on Earth. Walk to the top of the hill — the view from there is what you want: endless color stepping down toward the ocean. A small train takes you around the perimeter if the paths get hot. Go on a weekday morning, buy a cup of coffee and a lemon scone at the little stand near the entrance, and take the path that leads into the rose garden tucked inside the larger field. Ranunculus don't have fragrance, which surprised me the first time — they're all color. But the color! Rows and rows of tight, papery layers, each flower looking like something folded by hand. Stay for the sunset. The light from the ocean turns everything gold, even the flowers that are already gold.

## 6. Tulips, Daffodils, Hyacinths — Keukenhof, Lisse
*(approx 184 words · ~74 sec)*

Keukenhof is the largest spring garden in the world — seven million bulbs planted by hand each autumn, eight hundred varieties, thirty-two hectares of designed beauty. The gardens open for only eight weeks a year, March to May, and then they close again for the bulbs to rest. Book the earliest entry slot. The park empties out at lunch when tour buses pull away, but if you're inside at 8am you'll have the first hours to yourself. The Juliana pavilion at the back holds the rarest varieties — historical tulips from the Ottoman Empire, delicate species tulips, the famous striped Rembrandts that once caused a financial bubble in 17th-century Holland. Rent a bicycle and take the Bollenstreek route through the surrounding fields — commercial tulip farms where the color is shocking, unsubtle, endless. Buy raw herring from a stall, or a cone of hot stroopwafels. The Dutch love this season like no other country loves anything, and you can feel it in every small pleasure they offer you here. Bring a light rain jacket. The Netherlands is generous with its flowers and its weather.

## 7. Hyacinths — Lisse Fields, Netherlands
*(approx 187 words · ~75 sec)*

The Bollenstreek — the Dutch bulb region — is mostly known for its tulips, but in March and early April it belongs to the hyacinths. The fields around Lisse turn into striped bands of deep purple, pale blue, pink, and white, and the smell — oh, the smell. Hyacinth fragrance is heavy and sweet and drifts for miles on the wind; you smell the fields before you see them. Rent a bicycle from Leiden in the morning and cycle the flower route north — it's flat, well-signed, and passes farm after farm, each with a little stand selling flowers by the bunch. Stop for coffee and apple cake at one of the village cafés. The Dutch call this time bollenseizoen, bulb season, and they take it seriously — every farmhouse has a bouquet in the window, every window box is full. Hyacinths only last a few weeks before they're cut down to let the bulbs grow for next year, so come early in the season when the fields are at their full height. The color of an entire field of hyacinths is a color you don't forget.

## 8. Tulips — Tulip Festival, Holland, Michigan
*(approx 180 words · ~72 sec)*

Holland is a small city in western Michigan founded by Dutch immigrants in 1847, and it takes the Dutch tulip tradition seriously. Six million tulips bloom across the town every May — in public parks, along streets, around the DeZwaan windmill that was shipped over from the Netherlands in 1964 and is the only authentic working windmill in the United States. The annual Tulip Time Festival includes Dutch dances, klompen wooden-shoe parades, and fresh stroopwafels sold out of little carts. Veldheer Tulip Gardens has the best organized displays — 30 acres with six million tulips arranged by color and variety — and Windmill Island Gardens, where the DeZwaan turns, gives you the full Dutch village experience. The lakes here are still cold in May, but the tulips don't mind. Base yourself in downtown Holland at one of the small inns, walk the paved flower path along the lakeshore, and eat a proper Dutch breakfast of bread, cheese, and coffee at the Wooden Shoe diner. It's a postcard-small town, but it does this season with a joy that feels fully earned.

## 9. Bluebonnets — Texas Hill Country
*(approx 187 words · ~75 sec)*

For a few weeks in late March and early April, the Texas Hill Country turns a color that Texans will argue about forever — the bluebonnet blue, a deep violet-blue that's neither quite blue nor quite purple, but is entirely, undeniably, Texas. The Lupinus texensis is the state flower, and it grows in wide drifts along the shoulders of Highway 290 between Fredericksburg and Johnson City, in pastures and along fence lines and across whole hillsides. Drive the back roads slowly. Pull over where other cars have pulled over — that's the local etiquette — and walk carefully into a patch for a photograph. Texans have a long tradition of photographing their babies in bluebonnets, and you'll see families doing this, kids in small cowboy hats sitting very seriously in the flowers. Fredericksburg is the town to base in: German heritage, small bakeries selling apple strudel, a long main street of peach orchards and wineries. Come for a weekend, go wildflower hunting in the morning, eat brisket for lunch, and end the day at one of the small wineries with a view over the hills still blooming.

## 10. Sunflowers — Val d'Orcia, Tuscany
*(approx 197 words · ~79 sec)*

The Val d'Orcia is the Tuscany you've seen in every movie — cypress-lined roads, stone farmhouses on hilltops, slow turns of land unfolding toward more hills. Sunflowers are a summer crop here, and in June and July the rolling fields turn gold, wave after wave, against the darker green of the cypresses. The road between San Quirico d'Orcia and Pienza has the most photographed fields, and the road toward Bagno Vignoni runs past smaller patches tucked into valleys. Tuscan farmers grow sunflowers mostly for oil — girasole — and the plants follow the sun through the morning and then face east again at evening. The bloom itself lasts only two or three weeks before the heads droop and the seeds set. Stay in one of the agriturismi — working farms that take guests — and wake early for the best light. Sip an espresso on the terrace. The hills are soft gold in the first sun, deeper gold by mid-morning, and by noon everything washes out in the heat. Eat a long Tuscan lunch of pici and pecorino, nap in the afternoon like a sensible person, and go back out at sunset when everything turns to honey.

## 11. Lavender — Cotswolds Lavender, Snowshill
*(approx 201 words · ~80 sec)*

Cotswolds Lavender near Snowshill village grows its lavender in long, orderly rows that crest over a small hill and disappear toward the horizon of honey-colored stone villages. The English do lavender differently from the French — tidier, smaller scale, more gardened — and here the effect is the quintessential Cotswolds postcard. Walk through the fields for the scent and the hum of bees, then take the short stroll into Snowshill itself, one of the prettiest villages in the region — pub, manor house, church with a modest garden. Visit the farm shop for lavender scones, lavender shortbread, lavender lemonade (don't skip it — it's actually good), and the essential oil distilled on-site. Lavender season here runs from late June through the end of July. Go on a weekday if you can. The English countryside in summer is deeply, almost gently beautiful — the kind of landscape that has been cared for continuously for a thousand years. Combine a visit to Cotswolds Lavender with stops at Stow-on-the-Wold, Bourton-on-the-Water, and Chipping Campden. Have afternoon tea somewhere with a view. Bring a light cardigan; English summer evenings cool off quickly. Take a small bottle of the oil home. It smells like a whole valley.

## 12. Bluebells — Hallerbos Forest, Halle
*(approx 191 words · ~76 sec)*

Hallerbos is a small beech forest south of Brussels, and for about three weeks in April its floor turns completely purple. Wild bluebells — Hyacinthoides non-scripta — come up by the millions through last year's leaves, and because the beech canopy hasn't yet leafed out, the sunlight falls straight down through the bare branches onto this layer of intense, saturated color. It looks like someone has laid a purple carpet through the whole forest. The park has marked walking trails and asks you to stay on them — trampling the bluebells damages next year's bloom. Follow the main loop, which takes you through the densest patches, and if you have time add the longer trail through the younger forest where the flowers mix with wild anemones and yellow wood-sorrel. Go on a weekday. The weekends, especially in peak, draw crowds from all over Belgium and the Netherlands. Bring a camera — the light at mid-morning, filtered through the bare beech branches, is the best you'll get anywhere. The forest has no café, so pack a small picnic and eat somewhere quiet under the trees, the bluebells reaching up around your feet.

## 13. Alpine Wildflowers — Olympic National Park, Washington
*(approx 192 words · ~77 sec)*

Olympic National Park in Washington holds several small worlds — rainforests on one side, high subalpine meadows on the other. In July and August, the meadows at Hurricane Ridge fill with avalanche lilies, Indian paintbrush, lupine, beargrass, and glacier lilies. From the ridge you look out across the Olympic Mountains on one side, the Strait of Juan de Fuca on the other, and Vancouver Island beyond that. Hurricane Ridge is the most accessible meadow — a paved road goes right to it. For fewer people, drive farther along the gravel Obstruction Point Road; the meadows there are just as spectacular and almost always empty. The wildflowers here are short-lived: snow melts late, and summer is compressed into eight weeks. Check the park's meadow bloom report before you go. Deer and marmots come close; black bears are possible but not a real worry on the main trails. Pack a proper picnic — bread, cheese, a thermos of coffee — and eat it on one of the benches at Hurricane Ridge looking at the glaciers across the valley. The Pacific Northwest does alpine the way few places do. You leave with your lungs clean.

## 14. Alpine Wildflowers — Crested Butte, Colorado
*(approx 201 words · ~80 sec)*

Crested Butte calls itself the Wildflower Capital of Colorado, and in July it earns the title. The high meadows above town fill with columbine, lupine, Indian paintbrush, mule's ear, larkspur, fireweed, and a hundred other Rocky Mountain natives. The annual Wildflower Festival in mid-July offers guided walks, flower photography workshops, and talks from local botanists. The Judd Falls trail is short and easy and passes through some of the densest patches; Rustler Gulch is a longer, harder hike that takes you deep into a flower-filled basin with waterfalls. Come in early to mid-July. The bloom varies year to year — snowpack determines when things open — but most summers peak in the first two weeks of July. Crested Butte itself is a Victorian mining town turned ski resort turned summer paradise, with ice cream shops, a good brewpub, and a strong sense of itself. The elk bugle in September but the meadows bloom in July. Stay a few nights. Hike early in the morning before the afternoon thunderstorms roll in off the mountains. Eat a big breakfast afterward — huevos rancheros or a proper American diner plate. The Rockies in summer are one of the great secrets of North American travel.

## 15. Lupins — Lake Tekapo
*(approx 204 words · ~82 sec)*

Lake Tekapo sits in the middle of New Zealand's South Island — a glacial lake so turquoise it looks unreal, ringed by brown-gold tussock hills and snow-capped peaks. In late November and December, Russell lupins cover the lake's shores in spires of purple, pink, yellow, and blue. The Church of the Good Shepherd, a tiny stone chapel built in 1935, stands right at the water's edge. A photograph of the church with lupins in the foreground and the turquoise lake behind is one of New Zealand's iconic images. Go at sunrise. Lupins are technically an invasive species here — they've spread aggressively since being introduced — so don't let any New Zealander hear you celebrate them too loudly. Stay overnight in Tekapo village. The night skies above the lake are a designated International Dark Sky Reserve, and the Milky Way runs straight over the summits of the Southern Alps like a ribbon. The Mount John Observatory has guided astronomy tours. Eat breakfast at the café on the hill, with lupins out one window and the lake out the other. Drive south to Mount Cook the next day; the road passes lupin-edged lakes and rivers the whole way. New Zealand knows how to surprise you.

## 16. Jacaranda — Buenos Aires
*(approx 197 words · ~79 sec)*

In November, Buenos Aires turns purple. The city has over 20,000 jacaranda trees, planted throughout the 20th century, and when they bloom simultaneously they form a canopy above boulevards, plazas, and sidewalks that is one of the great urban floral displays. Plaza San Martín is a classic spot, with the old British clock tower rising behind the trees, and Avenida Santa Fe between Recoleta and Palermo is the most photographed stretch. The trees shed their leaves before flowering, so the purple is unbroken — the whole canopy floats unattached to any green. Purple petals carpet the sidewalks, cling to car windshields, collect in the gutters. It's messy, and Buenos Aires loves every bit of it. November weather is warm and dry, perfect for walking. Eat long lunches. Do the late dinners. Go to a milonga and watch Argentines tango until 2 am. Stop at the old Paris-style cafés — Café Tortoni, La Biela, Las Violetas — for coffee under ceiling fans. Walk the Rose Garden in Palermo, which is at its peak the same time as the jacarandas. Buenos Aires is one of the great walking cities, and the jacaranda season turns every walk into something memorable.

## 17. Roses — International Rose Test Garden, Portland
*(approx 192 words · ~77 sec)*

Portland calls itself the City of Roses, and the International Rose Test Garden is the reason. Ten thousand rose bushes, six hundred and fifty varieties, the oldest continuously operating public rose test garden in the United States — founded in 1917 as a sanctuary for European roses during World War I. The garden sits on a hillside in Washington Park with a view across downtown Portland to Mount Hood in the distance. Go at sunset. The low light turns the white roses almost golden, and Mount Hood stands pink at the horizon. Admission is free — Portland is generous like that. Wear comfortable shoes; the garden is larger than it looks, with terraces stepping down the hillside. Stop at the Japanese Garden next door afterward for tea and the raked-gravel calm. Portland in June smells like roses the way Kyoto in April smells like cherries. Even the supermarket parking lots have rose bushes. The test garden holds old roses from the Middle Ages and brand-new hybrids being trialed for commercial release. Bring your nose. The modern roses are stunning, but the heritage roses are the ones that make you close your eyes.

## 18. Mixed Exhibition Flowers — Chelsea Flower Show, London
*(approx 183 words · ~73 sec)*

The Chelsea Flower Show is the grand dame of the world's garden exhibitions — five days each May in the grounds of the Royal Hospital in London, where the best gardeners on Earth spend months building show gardens only to dismantle them at the end of the week. Every national newspaper covers it. The Queen used to walk through at the official preview, and it's still considered the most prestigious horticultural event anywhere. Go on the last day, Saturday, when exhibitors sell off their plants and flowers in what is politely called the sell-off and much less politely fought over. You can leave with armloads of rare roses, mature shrubs, whole display arrangements, at steep discounts. The Great Pavilion alone — a 12,000-square-meter tent — is worth the entry, with nurseries from every county of the UK showing their best. Tickets sell out months in advance. Wear comfortable shoes. Eat at one of the posh food stalls. And take notes. You'll come away with a garden's worth of ideas and at least one plant you didn't know you wanted but now absolutely need.

## 19. Dahlias — Conservatory of Flowers, San Francisco
*(approx 199 words · ~80 sec)*

San Francisco's Conservatory of Flowers is the oldest Victorian glass greenhouse in North America, a white wedding cake of a building that has been tended in Golden Gate Park since 1879. Most tourists visit the conservatory itself for the orchids and tropicals. Almost no one notices that just behind it is the Golden Gate Park Dahlia Garden — 600+ dahlia plants in an open-air display that's one of the best urban dahlia gardens in the United States. The dahlia is San Francisco's official flower, and the garden volunteers tend it through the summer, with the blooms peaking from September through mid-October. The varieties are staggering — dinner-plate dahlias the size of a face, pom-poms, cactus-formed, bicolored. Walk through in the early morning; the fog often hangs heavy in the park until mid-day. Bring coffee. The Japanese Tea Garden next door opens at the same time and is even more beautiful in the fog. San Francisco's microclimate means the dahlias bloom later and longer than almost anywhere else — a small perk of its famously cold summers. Don't miss the dahlia garden. It is one of those quiet civic pleasures that feels handmade, tended by volunteers because they love it.

## 20. Southern Magnolia — Forsyth Park, Savannah, Georgia
*(approx 210 words · ~84 sec)*

Forsyth Park sits in the heart of Savannah's historic district, 30 acres of Spanish moss-draped live oaks and grand old magnolias. The Southern magnolia — Magnolia grandiflora — blooms in late spring, with cream-white flowers the size of dinner plates that release a lemony, heavy perfume across the whole park. The white marble fountain at the park's center, modeled on the Place de la Concorde in Paris, reflects flowering trees and walkers moving slowly through the heat. Magnolias are the iconic tree of the American South, and Savannah wears them with full ceremony. Combine a visit with a walking tour of the historic squares — Savannah has 22 of them, each a pocket park surrounded by 19th-century architecture — and a cup of sweet tea on a shaded porch somewhere. Stop at the Gryphon Tea Room or Leopold's ice cream. Eat shrimp and grits at a small restaurant tucked into an alley. The magnolia blooms from April through June, with peak in May. Savannah's spring has an almost impossible density of flowering trees — wisteria on wrought-iron balconies, dogwoods under the oaks, azaleas everywhere — but the magnolia is the one that signals, perfume first, that summer is on its way. Pack a book. Find a bench. Sit a while.

## 21. Tulips — Parc de l'Indépendance, Morges
*(approx 209 words · ~84 sec)*

The small Swiss town of Morges sits on the north shore of Lake Geneva, about twenty minutes from Lausanne, and every spring its Parc de l'Indépendance fills with 120,000 tulips of every color, arranged in elegant beds along the lakefront. The annual Tulip Festival — Fête de la Tulipe — runs from late March through early May, and the park becomes one of the most photogenic spots in western Switzerland: tulips in orderly Swiss rows, Lake Geneva behind, the French Alps on the far shore across the water. Morges itself is a small lakeside town with a castle, a medieval old town, and cafés along the promenade. Walk the park, then walk the waterfront to the Château de Morges. Stop for a glass of Chasselas, the local white wine, at one of the cafés facing the lake. The Swiss take tulips as a quieter affair than the Dutch — smaller scale, neater, framed by mountains and still water rather than vast flat polder. In April, cherry blossoms also flower in the town, and on a clear day you can see Mont Blanc on the horizon. Take the slow train along the lake. This is Switzerland at its soft-spring best — neat, elegant, and almost too beautiful to be true.

## 22. Sunflowers — Valensole Plateau, Provence
*(approx 212 words · ~85 sec)*

The Valensole Plateau is famous for lavender, but in mid-July — sometimes into early August — the same fields that held lavender a few weeks before come into sunflower bloom. The Provençal farmers rotate crops, and for a few weeks you can see both: ribbons of dark purple still lingering in some fields, and shoulder-high sunflowers waving gold in others, against the same sky and the same distant limestone hills. The road between Valensole and Manosque passes the best concentration. Pull over at any of the marked stopping points. Fields of sunflowers — tournesols — track the sun through the morning and turn east at evening. The combination of lavender and sunflower is what makes Valensole in late July so specific — two of the great Provençal flowers overlapping briefly. The light here is the light that painters have been drawn to for two centuries — thick, warm, golden. Eat a long lunch at a village bistro. Try the local honey, now perfumed with both flowers. Drink a glass of rosé from a Provençal vineyard. Visit the lavender distillery if it's still open. The plateau has a gentle, agricultural calm — working farmland that happens to be beautiful — and its summer colors make a case for slow travel through southern France.

## 23. Desert Wildflowers — Anza-Borrego Desert State Park, California
*(approx 209 words · ~84 sec)*

Anza-Borrego Desert State Park is the largest state park in California — 600,000 acres of desert in the southern Colorado Desert — and in good rain years it produces one of the great wildflower superblooms of North America. In February and March, after a wet winter, the flat desert floor, the sloping alluvial fans, and the narrow washes fill with desert sand verbena, desert lilies, dune evening primrose, chuparosa, desert sunflowers, and the Borrego Palm Canyon's own unique flora. The park has a real-time wildflower hotline — call it before you go. Base yourself in the small town of Borrego Springs. Drive the back roads at dawn. Walk Henderson Canyon Road for sheets of purple sand verbena, and Coyote Canyon Road for the best desert lilies. Anza-Borrego does not do superblooms every year — it takes a particular sequence of autumn and winter rains — but when it happens, the desert becomes a watercolor you wouldn't have thought possible. The stars here at night are unbelievable; Anza-Borrego is an official Dark Sky Park. Eat a date shake at Shields Date Garden in nearby Indio. Go to Salvation Mountain. The Colorado Desert is one of the quieter, less-visited corners of the American West, and its flower years make it unforgettable.

## 24. Plumeria — Maui, Hawaii
*(approx 173 words · ~69 sec)*

On Maui, plumeria grows like it's been waiting for you. You'll find it at the side of the road, spilling over garden walls, dropped onto the sidewalks by children — white, yellow, deep pink, sometimes all three on the same tree. The smell is like nothing else: sweet but not heavy, a little peppery, the kind of fragrance you catch on a breeze and try to find again. Kula Botanical Garden, up in the cool hills, has the most diverse plumeria collection on the island, with varieties brought from all over the Pacific. Take a slow drive up there in the morning when the air is still cool, and plan to spend an hour just walking between trees. In Hawaii, plumeria is the flower of welcome. Women tuck it behind one ear when they're single, behind the other when they're spoken for. Buy a lei from one of the stands near the airport — not the plastic kind, the fresh one that smells like the whole island — and wear it all day.

## 25. Neelakurinji — Eravikulam National Park, Munnar
*(approx 200 words · ~80 sec)*

This one is magic — a flower that blooms only once every twelve years. The neelakurinji is a small blue-violet shrub that grows on the high hills of the Western Ghats, and when it flowers, the entire Eravikulam National Park above Munnar turns blue-violet. It's so rare that generations of Malayali poets have written about it. The next big bloom is expected in 2030 — worth marking the calendar for. The Muthuvan tribal people who live in these hills traditionally counted their age in neelakurinji blooms. Even outside the mass flowering year, you can visit Eravikulam in August and September to see smaller patches and to look for the nearly-extinct Nilgiri tahr, the small mountain goat that still grazes these slopes. Munnar itself is a tea country town — green tea estates stepping down the valleys, the air thin and cool, small chai shops on every corner. Take a long drive through the estates in the morning when the mist is still rising. Eat a plate of puttu and kadala curry for breakfast. The trip is gentle: a flight to Kochi, a winding drive up into the hills, a few days in a tea plantation guesthouse, waiting for something blue.

## 26. Bougainvillea — Jaipur, Rajasthan
*(approx 208 words · ~83 sec)*

Jaipur in the dry winter months is wrapped in bougainvillea. The pink, magenta, orange, and white papery flowers cascade over the walls of the old city, over the arches of the Hawa Mahal, over the windowsills of haveli guesthouses, over the stone bases of Nahargarh Fort looking down on everything. The Rajput architects of three centuries ago didn't design with bougainvillea in mind — the plant was introduced later — but it has adopted their rose-pink walls and fluted pavilions as if it were always meant to be here. Walk the old city at late afternoon, in the golden hour, when the light catches the flowers and the pink walls and everything glows. Stop at the Amber Fort overlook for the classic view. Eat a thali at LMB in Johari Bazaar. Buy a jar of pickle, a length of block-printed cotton. The rooftop cafés behind the City Palace serve chai and curries with the whole pink city spread below. Bougainvillea needs heat, sun, and very little water — perfect for the dry Rajasthan winter — and it blooms almost continuously from October through March. An easy weekend: a morning train from Delhi, three days in Jaipur, a slow breakfast every morning on a haveli rooftop, and bougainvillea everywhere.
